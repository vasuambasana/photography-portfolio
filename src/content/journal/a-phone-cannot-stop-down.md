Ask the archive what aperture each lens was used at and the answer splits cleanly in two.
The Canon RF 70-200mm appears at f/2.8, f/3.2, f/5, f/6.3 and f/11. Every Samsung lens here
appears at exactly one: the S22 Ultra's main camera always at f/1.8, the S24 Ultra's always
at f/1.7, the telephotos always at f/2.4, f/3.4 or f/4.9, each fixed to its own. There is no
iris in these lenses to close.

That matters most when there is too much light. A camera with an iris can stop down and
keep everything else where it was. A phone has two moves only. It drops the ISO as low as
the sensor will go, and then it shortens the shutter.

[*Quiet Hilltop Sanctuary*](/photo/quiet-hilltop-sanctuary) is a wooden gazebo on top of a
grassy, sunlit hill under deep blue sky and streaks of white cloud. S24 Ultra, 23mm, f/1.7,
1/1500s at ISO 12. ISO 12 is the lowest figure anywhere in this archive, and eight frames
reach it. Had the lens been able to close to f/11, the same light would have allowed about
1/36s. Instead it sits at 1/1500s, because that was the only direction left.

[*Harbor Rhythm*](/photo/harbor-rhythm) is the same day and the same phone, on the 3x lens:
a small motorboat crossing a harbour in black and white, trailing a wake, the city massed
behind it under streaked cloud. f/2.4, 1/2500s at ISO 50. At that speed the wake is frozen
into a hard white shape. In that light there was no way to smear it into a soft line, short
of putting a filter in front of the lens.

[*Granite and Green*](/photo/granite-and-green) goes further: the S22 Ultra's ultrawide at
13mm, f/2.2, 1/2700s at ISO 50, with the sun in the frame above granite boulders
scattered across green hills. With the sun itself in the frame it is no surprise that it
is the fastest of the four.

[*After the Rain*](/photo/after-the-rain) is the quiet one. Broad hosta leaves close up,
covered in beads of water, on the S22's main camera at f/1.8 and ISO 12. It is at 1/800s,
the slowest of the four, and it is also the only one of them that is not about the sky.

The other thing an iris does is control depth of field, and a phone does not need much help
there. The sensor is small and the lens is short, so most of a wide frame is sharp whether
you like it or not. Stopping down would not change these pictures much. What it would change
is the shutter speed, and with it the option of letting anything blur.

For contrast, [*Distant Blue Ridges*](/photo/distant-blue-ridges) is the Canon at 70mm and
f/11, the narrowest aperture anywhere in this archive and more than two stops beyond the
narrowest fixed aperture on any of the phones.
