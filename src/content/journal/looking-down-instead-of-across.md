Street photography usually happens at eye level, close enough that people know you're there.
The five frames from this day are the opposite. Every one looks down from somewhere high, at
people who had no idea there was a camera above them.

Canon R5 Mark II, ISO 200 throughout. Two lenses doing two different jobs: the RF 35mm for
the wider frames and the 70-200mm for the ones where the subject is small and far below.

## What height does to a frame

From above, a scene stops being a space you're standing in and becomes a pattern you're
reading. The horizon disappears. There's no sky to balance against, no background behind
anyone's head — just surfaces, and whatever is standing on them.

[*Captured in the Geometry*](/photo/captured-in-the-geometry) is the clearest version. Looking straight down onto an empty
restaurant deck: weathered grey boards running in three directions, wooden tables, white
wicker chairs pushed in, one blue chair against a white clapboard wall. Almost all of it
unoccupied.

In the middle of that emptiness, a couple stand with their arms around each other, holding a
phone up at the end of an outstretched arm, taking a picture of themselves.

Shot at 115mm, 1/400s. They never looked up. From directly overhead the deck reads as a flat
composition of rectangles, and the two of them are the only curved, unaligned thing in it —
which is the whole reason the frame works.

## The other one with people in it

[*Patterns of Solitude*](/photo/patterns-of-solitude), at 70mm and 1/500s, is the same idea with more room to breathe.

Palms and coarse grass planted in raked sand, a boardwalk cutting a hard diagonal across the
top of the frame, a wooden bench tucked into a corner. On the bench, two people sitting close
together, heads bent toward each other. Up and to the left, unconnected to them, a man walks
alone across the open sand.

Two kinds of solitude in one frame — the shared kind and the actual kind — and the only
reason both fit is that the camera is far enough above to hold them in the same rectangle.
At eye level they'd have been two entirely separate photographs.

## The ethics of it, briefly

Photographing people from above who don't know you're there is easier than doing it at eye
level, and I'm not sure "easier" is a recommendation.

What I'd say in its defence: nobody in these frames is identifiable in any meaningful way.
From that height and that angle you get the tops of heads, posture, the shape of how people
arrange themselves near each other. The couple on the deck are a couple; they aren't anyone
in particular. That distinction is the thing worth holding onto, and it's also exactly what
makes the pictures work — they're about proportion, not about individuals.

If a frame only works because you recognise the person in it, and they didn't know, that's a
different photograph and I'd think harder about it.

## The rest of the day

The remaining three run wider. [*Coastal Quietude*](/photo/coastal-quietude) and [*Playing with the Past*](/photo/playing-with-the-past) are both at
35mm — 1/8000s and 1/3200s, which is a lot of shutter for ISO 200 and says how hard the light
was. [*A Quiet Afternoon Aboard*](/photo/a-quiet-afternoon-aboard) sits between the two modes at 111mm.

Looking at the five together, the day has an obvious shape: find height, point downward, wait
for someone to walk into the geometry. It isn't a method I use often. It worked here because
the vantage existed and I stayed put long enough to use it properly, which is usually the
harder half.
