Most people avoid the coast in February, and honestly, standing out there on the rocks, it's pretty hard to argue with them. 

The thermometer in my car read nineteen degrees when I pulled into the empty turnout. By the time I walked down the pine path toward the water, the wind coming off the bay had cut right through my fleece-lined pants. The rocks were coated in a thin sheen of salt ice that looked like wet slate until you put a boot on it and almost ended up in the tide pools.

The sky had been a flat, depressing steel-gray all afternoon. No texture, no gradients, just a solid slab of winter overcast. I set my pack down on a relatively dry slab of granite and stood there for forty minutes with my hands jammed into my coat pockets, wondering why I didn't stay home with a cup of coffee.

Then, around 4:40 PM, the very bottom of the cloud deck began to fray.

It didn't clear out. The sky never went blue. But a gap opened up right above the water line, maybe three finger-widths wide, and the sun dropped through it like a hot coin.

For about twenty minutes, the entire mood flipped. That harsh, freezing granite turned deep amber. The patches of frozen sea spray along the rocks lit up like crushed glass. The evergreens behind me went from muddy green to rich bronze, and the water took on this deep, heavy indigo that you only ever get when the ocean is nearly freezing.

I had to pull off my heavy outer gloves to work the shutter button and small dials on the camera body. Within ninety seconds my fingertips went numb, but you don't really register the stinging until afterward. You just work the frame. Keep the horizon level, watch the foam patterns rolling over the lower ledges, wait for the lighthouse out on the spit to catch that brief hit of warm side-light.

By 5:05 PM, the sun had slipped behind the western hills, the amber light vanished, and the coast instantly turned blue-black and bitter cold again. My fingers throbbed the whole thirty-minute drive back into town while the car heater struggled to catch up.

That's the trade-off with winter shooting up here. In July, you're fighting crowds, dodging tripods, and dealing with that muggy white haze that turns the sunset to mush. In February, you have the entire headland to yourself. If the light gives you five minutes, it's five minutes of clean, knife-sharp contrast that summer can't touch.
