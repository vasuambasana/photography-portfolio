Three frames survive from two April days in the mountains, all on a Galaxy S22 Ultra, and
the middle one is the reason I remember the trip.

[*Spectrum in the Mist*](/photo/spectrum-in-the-mist) is a waterfall coming down a sheer
granite cliff with a rainbow standing in the spray above the pines. It is at **230mm
equivalent**, f/4.9, 1/400s, ISO 40.

A rainbow in falling water only exists from one position. Move twenty feet and the angle
between the sun, the mist and your eye changes and it is gone. You cannot walk up to it and
you cannot wait for it to come to you, so either you have the reach or you do not have the
photograph. That the reach came from a phone's super-telephoto still seems slightly absurd
to me, and it is the second time in this archive that 230mm on a phone has done work I would
have assumed needed a long lens.

The two frames on either side of it are both at **ISO 12**, which is the floor this sensor
reports and means it had more light than it could use.
[*Clear Light over Yosemite*](/photo/clear-light-over-yosemite) is the wide one at 23mm and
1/1250s, granite face, waterfall, snow still on the peaks behind, nothing in the sky at all.
That is high-altitude spring light doing what it does, and there is not much skill in it.

[*Light after the Fire*](/photo/light-after-the-fire) is the next day, also 23mm and ISO 12,
at 1/380s. A slope of burnt bare trunks standing in snow with a warm sunset across them. The
slower shutter is the only clue that the light had dropped, and what makes the frame is that
the two things in it, fire damage and fresh snow, do not normally sit in the same picture.

Three frames, two days, one phone, and a spread from ISO 12 to ISO 40 which is barely a
spread at all. Mountain light in April is absurdly generous. The difficulty that trip was
never exposure, it was standing in the one place the rainbow existed from.
