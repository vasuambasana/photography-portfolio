Eleven photographs here were made at 230mm equivalent, every one of them on the Galaxy S22
Ultra's periscope lens at f/4.9. Five of them are pointed at something that is in the sky
and nowhere else: two airliners, two moons, and a line of pelicans.

[*Selenic Tapestry*](/photo/selenic-tapestry) is the moon close to full, filling the middle
of the frame, seas and craters resolved against black. 1/200s at ISO 40.

There is an old rule for the moon that predates all of this. Set f/11 and use a shutter
speed of one over the ISO. At ISO 40 that is 1/40s at f/11. This lens is stuck at f/4.9,
which lets in about five times as much light as f/11, so the same rule asks for about
1/200s. The file says 1/200s. Whether the phone got there by itself or I set it, the file
does not record, but the number is the right one.

The reason the rule works is that the moon is a rock in direct sunlight, and it should be
exposed like one. Which is why the two aircraft land in the same place.
[*Ascent into the Void*](/photo/ascent-into-the-void) is an airliner high up and lit white,
two long contrails running the full height of the frame behind it, at 1/350s and ISO 40.
[*Through the Canopy*](/photo/through-the-canopy) is another one seen through the bare
branches of a tree against deep blue, at 1/190s and ISO 40. The moon and both planes sit
within a stop of each other. Two years, three subjects, one light source.

In Through the Canopy the twigs in the foreground are sharp and the plane is soft. At 230mm
the depth of field is thin enough that only one of them could be sharp, and it is the
branches. The plane still reads as a shape, and the branches are what make the frame more
than a plane-spotting record.

The sky in Ascent into the Void is completely black, and that is the one number here I
cannot account for. At 1/350s and ISO 40 an ordinary daytime blue would still come out
blue, so the black is either a much darker sky than the exposure suggests or it was made
in the edit, and the file does not say which.

[*Framed in Shadow*](/photo/framed-in-shadow) is the other moon, and it goes the opposite
way. A thin crescent in a deep blue sky between the black silhouettes of leafy branches,
at 1/15s and ISO 640. That is nearly eight stops more exposure than the full moon got, and
the crescent is just a white sliver as a result. The exposure is for the sky and the leaves.
The moon is only there as the bright point the rest of the frame is arranged around.

[*In Parallel Motion*](/photo/in-parallel-motion) is the one that is not lit by anything
dramatic. A tall agave bloom stalk standing up the frame and a line of pelicans flying past
it in a column, against a muted blue sky. 1/60s at ISO 100, about three stops more exposure
than the moon frame, which fits a sky that was pale rather than bright.

What these five share is that nothing in them could be walked up to. Every subject was
somewhere I could not get to, and the only question was whether the lens reached. The
rainbow in [The Rainbow Was at 230mm](/journal/the-rainbow-was-at-230mm) is the same problem
on the ground.
