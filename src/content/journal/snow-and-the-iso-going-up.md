Three frames from two days in January, all on a Galaxy Z Fold7, all at f/1.7. The only thing
that changes between them is how much light there was, and the ISO tracks it exactly.

[*Rise from the Frost*](/photo/rise-from-the-frost) is the first, at 36mm equivalent,
1/120s, **ISO 40**. A big illuminated phoenix lantern standing on snow with dark conifers
behind it. ISO 40 at night looks like a mistake until you remember the subject is the light
source. The lantern is lit from inside and everything else in the frame is black, so the
camera meters for the bright thing and gets an easy exposure out of it.

[*Chinatown Gateway in Winter*](/photo/chinatown-gateway-in-winter) is the next night, 44mm
equivalent, 1/40s, **ISO 400**. A paifang gate in falling snow, string lights along the
roofline, flags under the inscription, wet road throwing all of it back. Ten times the ISO
and a third of the shutter speed, because now the subject is a whole street rather than one
lantern, and street lamps have to cover it.

[*Quiet after the Snow*](/photo/quiet-after-the-snow) is the same day at 23mm, and it is
**1/7s at ISO 3200**. A park walkway, empty benches, bare trees, fresh snow. Nothing in it
is lit at all.

A seventh of a second handheld is not a shutter speed anyone chooses. It is what is left
after the sensor has gone as far as it will go, and the only reason the frame holds together
is that a snowy park at night contains almost nothing that moves.

Forty to 3200 is six and a third stops, and the three frames sit almost exactly two stops
apart. That was not deliberate. Snow does that: it takes any light in the scene and hands
most of it back, so the difference between a lit object, a lit street and an unlit park is
much wider than it looks while you are standing in it.
