Six frames came out of three consecutive nights in July 2025, and every single one of them
is 23mm equivalent, f/1.8, twenty-five seconds, ISO 1600, on a Galaxy Z Fold6.

Not similar. The same, six times, across three nights.

That is not discipline on my part. It is the phone's astronomy mode, which decides the
exposure itself and gives you one option. You point it up, brace it against something, and
it takes twenty-five seconds. There is no dial to disagree with.

What I find interesting is how different the six look anyway.
[*Starlit Harvest*](/photo/starlit-harvest) is red barns under stars with the last orange of
dusk still on the horizon behind a treeline, so it was taken early, before the sky had fully
gone. [*Celestial Ascent*](/photo/celestial-ascent) from the same night is the Milky Way
standing straight up over a dark hill, with the sky graded green to yellow low down.
[*Starlit Sanctuary*](/photo/starlit-sanctuary) the following night has a ring of treetops
and a red head torch marking somebody at the bottom of the frame.

Same exposure, three quite different photographs, and the variable is entirely where I stood
and what time I got there.

There is an argument that a camera which makes the decision for you is a worse camera. On
these six nights it was not, because twenty-five seconds at ISO 1600 is roughly what I would
have chosen, and the time I did not spend choosing it went into walking further from the
car.

The limitation shows up at the edges. Twenty-five seconds at 23mm is long enough that stars
are beginning to trail and short enough that they are not trails yet, which is a compromise
rather than a decision. If I had wanted either extreme, points or arcs, the phone had no way
to give it to me.
