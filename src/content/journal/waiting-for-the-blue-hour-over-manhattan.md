If you stand on an observation deck or a Midtown rooftop around 7:15 on a clear September evening, you'll see a predictable cycle play out. 

Hundreds of people gather against the glass with phones up, waiting for the red ball of the sun to touch New Jersey. As soon as it slips behind the horizon, a collective sigh goes through the crowd. Within ten minutes, half of them turn around and start heading for the elevators to beat the dinner rush.

They leave right before the actual show starts.

During sunset, the dynamic range in a city like New York is basically impossible to balance naturally. The sky is blindingly bright, and the streets below are deep, muddy shadows. The buildings just look like massive black cardboard cutouts against a bright background.

The window I actually care about doesn't begin until twenty minutes later. Photographers call it nautical twilight, but in practice, it's just the moment when the declining natural light from the sky perfectly matches the luminance of the interior office lights across Midtown.

For about twelve minutes, from 7:35 to roughly 7:48 PM on this particular evening, the balance was dead even.

Suddenly the towers don't look like obstructions—they reveal their internal skeleton. You can see individual floor plates on the One Vanderbilt spire, the yellow desk lamps left on in legal offices fifty stories up, and the slow, crawling ribbon of red brake lights snaking down the avenues below. In the far distance, the Hudson River holds onto a pale violet reflection of the upper atmosphere, while the sky directly above the skyline shifts from burnt amber to cobalt.

What always catches me by surprise from high up is how quiet the city looks compared to how loud it actually is down on the asphalt. Down on 42nd Street, sirens are bouncing off the brick, brakes are screeching, and crosswalks are overflowing. Up here, it looks like a living circuit board that someone slowly turned on with a dimmer switch.

I braced my forearms against the cold metal railing to keep the frame dead steady at 1/50th of a second. No tripod allowed up there, so you hold your breath, squeeze the shutter gently, and let the city's own power grid do the lighting work for you.
