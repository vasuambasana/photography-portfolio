Two frames from one evening over Manhattan, both on a Galaxy S24 Ultra at 23mm equivalent
and f/1.7. Everything interesting about them is in the gap between their exposures.

[*Twilight over Manhattan*](/photo/twilight-over-manhattan) is 1/50s at ISO 40. A broad
orange band still sitting on the horizon over the river, the towers going dark, the streets
below already lit. There is enough daylight left that the sensor is barely working.

[*Neon Horizon over Manhattan*](/photo/neon-horizon-over-manhattan) is 1/30s at ISO 640.
Same phone, same lens, same view west. The orange has compressed into a thin line of
afterglow and the avenues underneath have become the brightest thing in the frame.

Between those two files the ISO goes up sixteenfold. That is the entire event. The sun
finishing its business, the city's own lighting taking over as the only source, and a small
sensor having to work a great deal harder for it.

Most people on a rooftop leave in the middle of that. The sun drops, everyone photographs
the sun dropping, and then a good proportion of them go and find dinner. The window they
walk out on is the one where the sky stops overpowering the buildings and the building
interiors start reading as light rather than as black rectangles.

At 1/50s handheld there is nothing keeping the frame steady except bracing against
something, and at 1/30s there is less than that. Both are well inside what a phone will
stabilise, which is the only reason both of them are usable at all.

The honest caveat is that this is one evening and two files. It is not a technique, and I
have not tested it anywhere else. What it does show is that the interesting part of a city
sunset is measurable: it is the stretch where the ISO climbs and nothing else about the
frame changes.
