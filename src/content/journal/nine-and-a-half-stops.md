Eight frames survive from five days in canyon country in April, and they came off three
different devices: a Canon R5 Mark II, a Galaxy Z Fold7, and an iPhone 17 Pro. I did not
plan that. It is just what was in reach each time.

Two of them were taken on the same day, both in daylight, and there are nine and a half
stops between them.

[*Echoes of the Canyon*](/photo/echoes-of-the-canyon) is the iPhone, 33mm equivalent at
f/1.8, 1/5300s at ISO 80. Someone in a straw hat sitting on a sandstone ledge with a
green river a long way below. A shutter that fast is not a decision anybody makes. It is a
camera reporting that the rim of a canyon at midday is one of the brightest things it will
ever be pointed at.

[*Light Through Sandstone*](/photo/light-through-sandstone) is the Canon, 35mm at f/2.8,
1/60s at ISO 1600. Smooth orange walls, deep purple shadow, light arriving from
somewhere above and bouncing its way down.

Work those two exposures back to a common ISO and the gap is nine and a half stops. The
inside of a slot canyon is roughly seven hundred times darker than the ledge above it, on
the same afternoon, within walking distance. The rock is doing all of it. There is no
weather involved, no time of day, just how much sky each place can see.

That is the thing I keep relearning about the desert. It reads as one enormous evenly lit
place and it is nothing of the sort, and none of that spread involves the sun going down.
Both frames are the middle of the day.

The night, by comparison, was easy. [*Cosmic Gateway*](/photo/cosmic-gateway) is a
twenty-five second exposure at ISO 500 on the Z Fold7, a blue-lit dome tent with a starry
sky over it and the last light still on a red ridge behind. Twenty-five seconds and ISO 500
is an unhurried exposure by any standard. Standing in the dark asked less of the camera
than standing in a canyon at noon.

The rest of the week is ordinary by comparison and I like it for that.
[*Golden Hour on the Red Rocks*](/photo/golden-hour-on-the-red-rocks) is 135mm at f/6.3,
the upper cliffs catching the last sun while the valley underneath has already gone.
[*Hikers on the Trail*](/photo/hikers-on-the-trail) is 70mm at f/5, a line of small figures
strung along a switchback, which is the only reason the wall behind them has any size at
all. [*Vastness of the Canyon*](/photo/vastness-of-the-canyon) is the same 70mm under heavy
overcast, and it is the weakest frame of the eight precisely because there is nobody in it.

Three cameras in five days is not a system. But the two frames that bracket the week were
taken hours apart on the same rock, and no single camera setting would have got both.
