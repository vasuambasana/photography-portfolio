The rule I learned was that your shutter should be at least one over the focal length. At
200mm that means 1/200s handheld, and below it you accept blur.

There are three frames here at 200mm and 1/60s. That is nearly two stops under the rule, and
all three hold.

Two of them are from the same January night, and their settings are identical: 200mm, f/2.8,
1/60s, ISO 2500, on the RF 70-200mm.
[*Beacon in the Void*](/photo/beacon-in-the-void) is the lit upper floors of the Prudential
Tower with its red mast above the roofline and the rest of the frame completely black.
[*Midnight Interlude*](/photo/midnight-interlude) is a black car at the kerb between a lit
brick wall and bare trees, with a runner going past on the pavement.

Same night, same settings, and the second one has a person moving in it. Stabilisation
cannot help with that. The runner is soft and the car is not, which is exactly what should
happen and is worth saying, because image stabilisation gets talked about as though it
freezes the scene. It does not. It holds the camera still and leaves the world to do
whatever it was doing.

[*Moonlit Tapestry*](/photo/moonlit-tapestry) is three weeks earlier at the same 200mm and
1/60s, this time at ISO 3200. A full moon burning through bare branches with thin cloud lit
around it. No lens is recorded on that file, so I cannot tell you which glass it was, only
the focal length.

What the three of them add up to is that the reciprocal rule is a rule about a camera
without stabilisation, and the camera is not that camera. Two stops of usable handheld
shutter is two stops of ISO I did not have to spend, and ISO 2500 instead of ISO 10000 is a
different photograph.

The honest limit sits right where the runner is. Below about 1/60s at this reach, everything
still-life holds and everything alive does not, and the decision stops being about my hands
and starts being about whether the subject is going to stand still.
