Six frames from one day in New York, all of them on a Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra. The Canon
stayed home.

The interesting part isn't that a phone took them. It's that all four of its cameras did,
ultrawide through super-telephoto, 13mm to 115mm equivalent, inside a single day. That's a
wider range than I own in glass.

[*Beacon of the Skyline*](/photo/beacon-of-the-skyline) is One World Trade Center at 115mm
equivalent, 1/1600s, ISO 32, shot from street level with tree canopy across the bottom of
the frame. At that reach the tower flattens into a geometric object. The chamfered corners
read as one long chevron running up the glass, the spire is a thin vertical line, and the
sky behind it is a bruised pink-grey. Nothing gives away the scale except the trees, and all
they tell you is that it's tall.

[*Verdant Sovereign*](/photo/verdant-sovereign) came off the same camera at the same 115mm
and the same ISO 32.

Those two are the ones people assume a phone can't do. What makes them work isn't the
sensor. It's that there was an enormous amount of light, and ISO 32 is a phone saying it has
some to spare.

After dark, [*Neon Pulse of the Metropolis*](/photo/neon-pulse-of-the-metropolis) switches to
the wide camera at 23mm equivalent, f/1.7, 1/60s, ISO 40, and it's the most crowded
photograph in this archive by a distance. Times Square looking down Seventh Avenue from the
red steps at Duffy Square, with the Father Duffy monument in the middle distance and its
inscription still legible. Billboards stacked up both sides, a screen reading HELLO SUMMER,
hundreds of people filling the bottom half, and a wedge of completely black sky over all of
it.

ISO 40 in Times Square isn't a mistake. The place throws out an absurd amount of light, and
the exposure problem is the inverse of normal night photography. The signage is brighter
than the people standing under it, and the whole frame turns into an argument about which of
the two you expose for.

[*Neon Current*](/photo/neon-current) goes to the telephoto at 69mm, 1/200s, ISO 100, and
pulls tighter into the same lit mess.

The ultrawide came out once, for [*Concrete Canopy under Stormy
Skies*](/photo/concrete-canopy-under-stormy-skies): 13mm equivalent, f/2.2, 1/60s, ISO 64.
Thirteen millimetres is a lens for standing underneath something and looking up. At 1/60s
it's far slower than the day's other daylight frames, which sat between 1/1250s and 1/2800s,
so the weather had clearly turned by the time I took it. [*Monolith and
Ribs*](/photo/monolith-and-ribs) closes the day out at 69mm and 1/2800s, the fastest shutter
of the lot.

Six photographs, four focal lengths, ISO between 32 and 100 on every one. That range would
have meant carrying two or three lenses, and instead it was one object that lived in a
pocket.

The limitation is real enough. All six were made in generous light, and none of this was a
dark restaurant or a dim gallery, which is where a small sensor starts complaining. Given
light, the phone wasn't what held the day back. Reach, weather and crowds were.

It's also the day I stopped apologising for phone frames in the archive. [*Beacon of the
Skyline*](/photo/beacon-of-the-skyline) hangs perfectly well next to anything off the Canon,
and nobody who has looked at it has asked what took it.
